Three Cities. Countless Voices.

Street art turns the city into a space for public expression. Across New York, Amsterdam, and São Paulo, artists have used walls, trains, and overlooked spaces to make themselves visible, tell community stories, and challenge who controls the urban landscape. Their histories reveal a movement shaped by both international exchange and local experience.

Late 1960s–1970s —

Claiming visibility

New York’s name-writing culture grows into an influential movement. In Amsterdam, graffiti develops alongside punk culture; São Paulo’s early street painters emerge during Brazil’s dictatorship. These scenes have distinct beginnings, united by artists taking expression into public space.

1980s —

Images cross borders

Photographs, exhibitions, and artist exchanges help graffiti circulate internationally. A direct connection appears in Amsterdam in 1984, when Henry Chalfant exhibits photographs of New York subway graffiti at Yaki Kornblit’s gallery.

1990s–2000s —

Expanding the possibilities

Street expression encompasses altered advertisements, paper portraits, murals, and small installations. Artists adapt techniques to their surroundings, creating recognizable local identities within an international movement.

2010s onward —

Recognition & Tension

Museums, commissioned murals, and cultural tourism give street art wider recognition. Yet questions remain about whose work receives protection, whose marks are erased, and how public art changes neighborhoods.
